Physical Models & Error Analysis

Dynamical Modelling and Validation of Long-Term LEO Trajectory Prediction

The force models, their governing equations, and the measured accuracy against Sentinel-3 precise orbits
Project: URSC — modular LEO orbit propagation & determination (Java, zero runtime dependencies)
Validation data: Sentinel-3A / Sentinel-3B POEORB precise orbits (GPS-derived POD), 16 Oct 2024, 10 s sampling
Document type: Physics & error-analysis report
Reference architecture: Orekit (independent clean-room implementation)
Abstract. This report documents the physics of a from-scratch orbital dynamics engine for low-Earth-orbit (LEO) trajectory prediction and the accuracy it achieves against independent, GPS-derived precise orbits. The equations of motion integrate a full perturbation stack: a spherical-harmonic geopotential (EGM96 to degree/order 70), luni-solar third-body attraction, atmospheric drag through the NRLMSISE-00 thermosphere, solar radiation pressure with a conical eclipse model, solid and ocean tides (IERS-2010 / FES2004), and post-Newtonian relativistic corrections, all referred to a rigorous GCRF↔ITRF transformation (IAU-2006/2000B precession, nutation, Earth rotation and polar motion). Validated against Sentinel-3 POEORB ephemerides, pure propagation from a single state vector reproduces the precise orbit with radial and cross-track errors held to a few metres over 24 h, while the along-track channel — the integrated signature of residual non-conservative mismodelling — grows to roughly 0.3 km. A batch least-squares fit reduces the 6-hour residual from ≈40 m to ≈5 m RMS. The results confirm that the conservative field is modelled correctly and localise the remaining error physically in the drag/ballistic channel.

1  Physical setting: the LEO perturbation environment

A satellite in low Earth orbit does not move on a closed Keplerian ellipse. The dominant central attraction is perturbed by a hierarchy of smaller forces whose relative importance is strongly altitude-dependent. The two validation targets, Sentinel-3A and Sentinel-3B, fly in a near-polar, Sun-synchronous orbit at roughly 800 km altitude (inclination ≈98.6°, near-circular), representative of Earth-observation LEO.

The purpose of the engine is twofold: (i) prediction — integrate the state forward over hours to years for trajectory and orbital-lifetime studies; and (ii) determination — recover the initial state and dynamical parameters that best reproduce a set of observations. Both rest on the same force models, so the physical fidelity of those models sets the ceiling on achievable accuracy.

Why POEORB is a strong reference. The Sentinel-3 POEORB product is a precise orbit determined on the ground from the satellite's onboard GPS receiver (dual-frequency POD), with a quoted accuracy of a few centimetres (radial < 3 cm). It is therefore effectively "truth" at the metre level relevant here, and — crucially — it is independent of our dynamics: it is a GNSS measurement product, not another propagation. This makes it an honest external check of the physics.

The central and J2 terms govern the gross geometry; everything below drag is conservative and cleanly periodic, which is why — as the validation shows — it maps into bounded radial and cross-track errors. Drag is the only large dissipative term, and it is the hardest to model because it depends on the state of the thermosphere.

3.2  Evaluation method

The potential gradient is computed with the Cunningham–Gottlieb recursion for the normalised potential derivatives (Montenbruck & Gill, Satellite Orbits, §3.2.4), which is numerically stable to high degree and avoids the explicit evaluation of Legendre polynomials and their derivatives. The field is static only in the Earth-fixed frame, so the acceleration is evaluated in ITRF and rotated back to GCRF using the frame transformation of §9. The expansion may be truncated independently in degree and order, allowing, for example, a zonal-only J2–J6 field (order 0) for secular studies or a full tesseral field for high-fidelity work.

5.1  Density model: NRLMSISE-00

Density is supplied by a pluggable atmosphere interface; the high-fidelity option is the NRLMSISE-00 empirical thermosphere model (a clean-room port of the reference C implementation, verified against the model's official test case to seven significant figures). It returns the effective total mass density for satellite drag (including anomalous oxygen above ~500 km) as a function of altitude, latitude, longitude, day-of-year, local solar time, and the solar/geomagnetic activity drivers:

  • F10.7 — the daily 10.7 cm solar radio flux (a proxy for EUV heating);
  • F10.7a — its 81-day centred average;
  • Ap — the daily planetary geomagnetic index.

These drivers enter through a space-weather provider that is either a constant (the validation used F10.7 = 220, F10.7a = 205, Ap = 12, representative of the active 2024 solar maximum) or a file-backed historical series (CelesTrak CSSI format). Lower-fidelity alternatives — a static exponential atmosphere and the diurnal Harris–Priester model — are also available for comparison and fast studies.

Physical caveat. Thermospheric density is uncertain at the 10–30% level even with a perfect activity input, because NRLMSISE-00 is a climatological average. Combined with the use of constant rather than true daily space-weather indices and a fixed cross-sectional area (the real satellite's attitude changes A), this is the principal reason the along-track error grows as it does in §10.

The (AU/d)² factor is the inverse-square fall-off of the flux.

6.1  Conical shadow model

The shadow factor ν is computed from a conical umbra/penumbra model using the apparent angular radii of the Sun and the occulting Earth as seen from the spacecraft, α⊙ = arcsin(R⊙/d) and α⊕ = arcsin(R⊕/r), and their angular separation. Full sunlight (ν = 1), total umbra (ν = 0), and the partial penumbra (computed from the lens-shaped overlap area of the two apparent discs, with the annular case handled separately) are all distinguished. This smooth shadow function is important physically: SRP switches on and off twice per orbit, and a sharp (cylindrical) shadow would inject spurious along-track impulses at each crossing.

9  Reference frames and time scales — the GNSS connection

Correctly relating the inertial integration frame (GCRF) to the Earth-fixed frame (ITRF) is essential: the geopotential and atmosphere are naturally expressed in ITRF, and — directly relevant to GNSS — the Sentinel-3 POEORB state vectors are given in ITRF. ITRF is the same realisation family in which GPS/GNSS products are expressed, so the frame chain below is exactly the transformation a GNSS positioning pipeline must also apply.

9.1  GCRF ↔ ITRF transformation

The full rotation follows the IAU-2006/2000B conventions as a chain of bias, precession, nutation, Earth rotation and polar motion:

(11)
  • B — frame bias (GCRF to the mean J2000 dynamical frame);
  • P(t) — precession via the IAU-2006 Fukushima–Williams angles;
  • N(Δψ,Δε) — nutation via the IAU-2000B 77-term luni-solar series (accuracy ~1 mas), with mean obliquity εA;
  • R3(GAST) — diurnal Earth rotation through the Greenwich Apparent Sidereal Time (mean sidereal time plus the equation of the equinoxes);
  • W(xp,yp) — polar motion (a small-angle wobble matrix).

10  Validation against Sentinel-3 precise orbits

The validation methodology is deliberately conservative and transparent. The propagator is seeded from a single POEORB state vector (the first epoch), transformed ITRF→GCRF with the file's own UT1, and integrated forward under the full force model with no fitting. At each subsequent epoch the propagated position is differenced against the precise orbit, and the error vector is decomposed into the local radial–along–cross (RTN) orbital frame:

Honest finding — drag-coefficient observability. The state-only fit is robust (residual collapses by 7–8× to ~5 m). However, the estimated drag coefficient is not physically meaningful over a 6-hour arc: it runs to 5.26 for S3A and even negative (−3.20) for S3B, against a physical expectation of CD ≈ 2.2. The reason is observability: over only ~4 revolutions the integrated drag signal is small and strongly correlated with the along-track components of the initial state, so the normal-equation system is ill-conditioned in the CD direction and the parameter absorbs unmodelled along-track error rather than true ballistic information. Reliable CD (or ballistic-coefficient) estimation requires multi-day arcs, and/or estimating a piecewise/empirical along-track acceleration, and proper observation weighting — see §13.

12.3  Accuracy in context

For a propagation seeded from a single state with no fit and climatological space weather, a 24-hour total error of ~0.15–0.17 km RMS at 800 km is a strong result: it corresponds to roughly 2 m of growth per ~100-minute revolution, i.e. the dynamics reproduce each orbit to about one part in 3×106 of the orbit circumference. The POEORB reference itself (~cm) is negligible in this budget, so the numbers above are the engine's own error, not the reference's. With a fitted arc the instantaneous accuracy is at the few-metre level (Table 4).

13  Conclusions and recommended physics refinements

The validation against independent, GNSS-derived Sentinel-3 precise orbits demonstrates that the conservative dynamics (geopotential, luni-solar attraction, tides, relativity) and the GCRF↔ITRF frame chain are correct at the metre level, and that the residual prediction error is physically localised in the drag/ballistic channel. The engine is therefore a sound basis for both long-term trajectory prediction and orbit determination. The following refinements, in physical priority order, would most improve accuracy:

  1. True space-weather drivers. Replace constant F10.7/Ap with the actual historical daily indices for the arc (CelesTrak/CSSI). This directly attacks the dominant density error and should substantially reduce the along-track growth.
  2. Ballistic/empirical estimation over long arcs. Estimate a ballistic scale factor (or a piecewise-constant / empirical along-track acceleration) over multi-day arcs, where CD becomes observable — resolving the ill-conditioning found in §11.
  3. Attitude-dependent area (box-wing). Replace the cannonball cross-section with a panel model for both drag and SRP, since the true projected area varies along the orbit.
  4. Higher-fidelity conservative models. Raise the geopotential to 70×70, adopt a JPL DE ephemeris for Sun/Moon, and add an IERS EOP file (polar motion + UT1 series) — each trims the few-metre radial/cross floor.
  5. Formal covariance. Propagate the estimation covariance to attach realistic uncertainty bounds to predictions (essential for conjunction/decay-window work).
  6. Direct GNSS measurement fit. Fit to the raw onboard GPS observations (the Sentinel-3 GNSSRD product) rather than the reduced POEORB positions, closing the loop with the GNSS data directly.
